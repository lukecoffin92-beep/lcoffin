Artifact 02 · Literature Review

The Positive and Negative Effects of Long Distance Running on Stress and Mental Health

A solo literature review synthesizing twelve peer-reviewed sources on when running helps mental health, and when high-volume competitive training starts to work against it.

Sports psychology Independent research 12 peer-reviewed sources
Contextual Statement

I wrote this literature review for a course that asked us to research a topic connected to our own lives, and the choice was easy: I'm a Division I distance runner at the University of Maryland, and I've spent years hearing that running is uncomplicated good for your mental health. Most of the time that's true for me. But I've also had stretches of training that left me exhausted, unmotivated, and harder to be around than I wanted to admit, and I wanted to know whether the research backed up what that actually felt like — not just the version of running people put on a poster.

Using PubMed and Google Scholar, I pulled twelve peer-reviewed sources on running, overtraining, and athlete mental health and compared what they found. The review is organized around four questions: why running tends to help mental health, how the competitive-athlete experience differs from the recreational-runner experience, what happens physiologically and psychologically when training outpaces recovery, and what the longer-term mental health risks look like for endurance athletes, including after their competitive careers end.

This artifact shows my ability to work with a real body of scholarly research — evaluating twelve sources for relevance and credibility, synthesizing findings across studies rather than summarizing them one at a time, and being honest about where the research is inconclusive rather than overstating what it shows. It also reflects something I care about outside of the writing itself: using research to take athlete mental health seriously, instead of treating burnout as a personal failure. That's part of why I built the companion infographics in Artifact 01 — this review is the evidence base; those are the version of it meant to actually reach a coach or a teammate.

Abstract

Most people think of running as a good way to improve mental health and deal with stress. A lot of research supports this idea and shows that running can improve mood, lower anxiety, and reduce symptoms of depression. At the same time, some studies have found that competitive runners can experience mental health challenges when training becomes too intense. Problems such as burnout, anxiety, emotional exhaustion, and depression have become more common topics in sports psychology research. This review looks at current research on both the positive and negative effects of long-distance running on mental health. The findings suggest that running can be very beneficial, but the effects depend on factors such as training volume, recovery, and competition level.

Introduction

When most people think about running, they usually think about the positive side of it. Running is often connected with stress relief, better mental health, and a healthier lifestyle. It is common to hear people say they go for a run when they need to clear their heads or take their mind off something stressful. Because of that, running has a reputation for being one of the best activities for improving both physical and mental health.

Running is one of the most popular forms of exercise worldwide, making it important to understand both its physical and psychological effects. As more people participate in recreational and competitive running, researchers have increasingly examined how training influences mental health.

Research on long distance running strongly supports this idea. Many studies have found that people who run regularly often report lower levels of stress, anxiety, and depression. Running can also help people feel more confident because they are constantly working toward goals and seeing progress. For some people, that goal might be finishing a race. For others, it could be running farther or faster than they did before. Either way, reaching goals often makes people feel good about themselves. Several studies also suggest that running can improve overall mood and help people manage everyday stress. For example, Oswald et al. (2020) and Pereira et al. (2021) both found that recreational running is associated with lower stress and improved mental well-being.

At first, I thought most of the research on this topic would focus only on those positive effects. After reading through the studies, I realized the topic is more complicated than I expected. While many researchers found that running improves mental health, others found that it can sometimes have negative effects, especially for athletes who train at a high level. Some studies discussed burnout, anxiety, depression, and emotional exhaustion among competitive runners and endurance athletes. This was interesting because running is usually seen as a solution to those problems, not something that could contribute to them.

One thing that came up in several studies was the difference between recreational runners and competitive runners. Someone who runs a few times a week for exercise is dealing with a very different situation than someone training for college races or major competitions. Competitive athletes often spend a large part of their day training and recovering. They also deal with injuries, pressure to perform, expectations from coaches, and the stress of competition. Because of that, the mental health effects of running may look very different depending on the type of runner being studied.

This topic is especially interesting to me because I am a Division I runner at the University of Maryland. Running has had a positive impact on my life in a lot of ways. It has helped me stay disciplined, manage stress, and work toward long-term goals. At the same time, there have been periods when training was physically and mentally exhausting. Reading through the research made me realize that many of the challenges discussed in these studies are things that competitive runners experience every day. That is one reason I wanted to learn more about what researchers have found.

The goal of this review is to examine current research on the positive and negative effects of long-distance running on stress and mental health. The paper looks at the mental health benefits of running, the stress experienced by competitive athletes, the effects of overtraining and burnout, and long-term mental health concerns. Looking at both sides of the issue provides a more complete understanding of how running affects different types of athletes.

Methods

The goal of this review was to better understand how long-distance running affects stress and mental health. Sources were found using databases such as PubMed and Google Scholar. Search terms included running and mental health, athlete burnout, overtraining syndrome, anxiety in athletes, depression in athletes, endurance athletes and stress, and recreational running psychology.

Articles were selected based on their connection to the topic and whether they came from peer-reviewed journals. Most of the sources were published within the last ten years, although a few older studies were included because they are frequently cited in sports medicine research. Twelve peer-reviewed scholarly sources were selected for this review. The articles were then compared to identify common findings, major trends, and areas where researchers still have questions.

Current Research and Findings

Mental Health Benefits of Running

One thing that showed up in almost every study was that running can have a positive effect on mental health. Even though the studies focused on different groups of people, many of them reached similar conclusions. Studies focusing specifically on recreational and long-distance runners found that runners tend to report lower stress levels and better overall mental health than people who do not run regularly.

Oswald et al. (2020) reviewed a large amount of research on running and mental health. Their review found that running is often connected to lower anxiety, lower depression levels, and better overall well-being. Many runners reported that running helped them deal with stress and gave them a healthy outlet when life became overwhelming. The researchers also found that running may improve life satisfaction and emotional health.

Pereira et al. (2021) found similar results when looking at recreational runners. Their review showed that many runners reported higher self-esteem, greater motivation, and improved mental well-being. One reason for this may be that running gives people clear goals to work toward. Whether someone is training for a race or simply trying to stay active, reaching those goals can create a sense of accomplishment. Together, the findings from Oswald et al. (2020) and Pereira et al. (2021) suggest that running consistently improves mental health across different groups of recreational runners, even though the studies examined slightly different populations.

Another interesting study looked at the personalities of marathon runners. Braschler et al. (2024) found that many long distance runners share traits such as determination, commitment, and resilience. These qualities may help runners deal with challenges both in sports and in everyday life. While the study does not prove that running creates these traits, it does show that many endurance athletes share similar characteristics.

Researchers have also tried to explain why running improves mental health. Some studies suggest that exercise increases chemicals in the brain that are connected to positive emotions and improved mood. Other researchers point to factors such as improved sleep, stress reduction, and increased confidence. The exact reason is probably a combination of several different factors rather than one simple explanation.

Another thing that stood out in the research was how often runners described a feeling of accomplishment. Running is a sport where progress can be measured fairly easily through distance, time, and consistency. Because of this, runners often have clear evidence that they are improving. That feeling of progress may be one reason so many people continue running even when training becomes difficult.

Stress and Mental Health in Competitive Athletes

Even though running can have many positive effects, several studies showed that competitive athletes face challenges that recreational runners often do not. Running for fun and running at a high level can be two very different experiences.

Rice et al. (2016) found that elite athletes experience many of the same mental health challenges as everyone else. Athletes can struggle with anxiety, depression, and stress despite being physically fit. The researchers also found that athletes deal with pressures that many people never experience, including competition, performance expectations, and fear of failure.

This finding makes sense because competitive athletes often spend a huge amount of time thinking about training and performance. For distance runners, races can feel like the final result of months of hard work. When things do not go as planned, it can be frustrating. Injuries can make things even more difficult because they prevent athletes from training and competing the way they want to.

Geiger et al. (2023) found that mental health symptoms in athletes are often connected. Athletes who reported anxiety frequently experienced other problems as well, including poor sleep, emotional distress, and trouble concentrating. This suggests that mental health challenges often affect more than one area of a person's life at the same time.

Gouttebarge et al. (2019) reported similar findings. Their review found evidence of anxiety, depression, emotional distress, and sleep problems among current and former elite athletes. One of the main points of their study was that mental health concerns should not be ignored simply because athletes appear healthy on the outside.

Several factors seem to increase stress among athletes. Injuries were mentioned in multiple studies, but researchers also discussed academic pressure, competition, and personal expectations. Many athletes place a lot of pressure on themselves to succeed, which can sometimes create additional stress even when nobody else is pressuring them.

Another thing that showed up in the research was athletic identity. Many competitive runners spend so much time training that running becomes a major part of who they are. Because of that, bad performances and injuries can have a bigger impact on confidence than many people might expect. Some researchers suggested that having interests and relationships outside of sports may help athletes deal with these challenges.

Overall, the research suggests that competitive running can still provide many mental health benefits, but it can also create additional stress. The experience of a competitive runner is often very different from the experience of someone who runs mainly for fitness or enjoyment. Understanding that difference is important when looking at how running affects mental health.

Overtraining and Burnout

One topic that came up a lot in the research was overtraining. Most runners understand that training is supposed to be challenging. The goal is to push the body so it can adapt and get stronger. The problem is that there is a point where more training is not always better. Several studies found that athletes who train too much without enough recovery may start experiencing both physical and mental health problems.

Kreher and Schwartz (2012) explain that overtraining syndrome happens when athletes continue training hard without giving their bodies enough time to recover. Common symptoms include fatigue, poor performance, irritability, mood changes, and a lack of motivation. One thing that stood out to me is that some of these symptoms can look similar to mental health problems. An athlete might think they are just tired or having a bad week when they are actually dealing with something more serious.

Meeusen et al. (2013) found that training stress is not necessarily a bad thing. In fact, athletes need some stress in order to improve. The issue is when the amount of stress becomes greater than the body's ability to recover from it. Their research showed that athletes who constantly push through fatigue without enough rest may increase their risk of overtraining syndrome. This is especially important in endurance sports where athletes often believe that doing more is always the answer.

Another topic that showed up in multiple studies was burnout. Gustafsson et al. (2017) describe burnout as a condition where athletes feel emotionally exhausted, lose motivation, and stop enjoying their sport. Instead of looking forward to practice, athletes may start dreading it. Some may even begin questioning whether they want to continue competing at all.

Burnout seems to develop over time rather than all at once. Several studies suggested that constant pressure, high expectations, and long periods of intense training can slowly wear athletes down. This makes sense because most athletes do not suddenly lose motivation overnight. Instead, stress builds up little by little until it becomes difficult to manage.

Several researchers suggested that many athletes may view burnout as a sign of weakness even though it is recognized as a legitimate issue in sports psychology. Researchers repeatedly pointed out that burnout is a real problem that can affect both performance and overall well-being. Because of that, many experts stress the importance of recovery, communication, and mental health support.

Overall, the research suggests that training stress can be positive when it is balanced with proper recovery. However, when athletes continue pushing themselves without enough rest, the risk of overtraining and burnout increases. This is one of the clearest examples of how something that is usually healthy can become harmful when taken too far.

Depression and Long Term Mental Health Concerns

Another major topic in the research was depression and other long-term mental health concerns among athletes. Most people assume that athletes are protected from depression because they exercise regularly. While exercise can help improve mental health, several studies showed that athletes can still struggle with depression and anxiety.

Forys et al. (2022) found that depression in athletes does not always look the same as it does in the general population. Some athletes continue training, competing, and performing well even while dealing with serious emotional struggles. Because they are still functioning at a high level, people around them may not realize anything is wrong. This can make it harder for athletes to get help.

Several studies, including Forys et al. (2022), Rice et al. (2016), and Gouttebarge et al. (2019), found that athletes may hide mental health struggles. In sports, there is often pressure to appear tough and mentally strong. Because of that, some athletes may avoid talking about anxiety or depression even when they are struggling.

Colangelo et al. (2023) focused on ultra endurance athletes and found evidence of anxiety, depression, eating disorders, and exercise dependence. Their research suggests that athletes who spend extremely large amounts of time training may face unique mental health challenges. This does not mean that every endurance athlete will experience these issues, but it does show that there can be risks associated with very high training volumes.

Researchers also discussed how injuries can affect mental health. For many athletes, training and competition are a major part of daily life. When an injury suddenly takes that away, athletes may feel frustrated, isolated, or uncertain about the future. Several studies suggested that injuries can increase the risk of anxiety and depression, especially when recovery takes a long time.

Another interesting area of research focused on athletes after retirement. Runacres et al. (2024) found that some former elite athletes continue to experience anxiety and depression even after their competitive careers end. This may happen because athletes often spend years building their identity around sports. When competition is no longer part of their lives, some struggle to adjust to a different routine.

Gouttebarge et al. (2019) found similar results in their review of current and former athletes. Their findings suggest that mental health concerns do not automatically disappear when someone stops competing. In some cases, challenges can continue for years after an athlete leaves their sport.

Overall, the research shows that mental health concerns among athletes deserve serious attention. Running and exercise can provide many benefits, but they do not make someone immune to anxiety or depression. Understanding that reality is important for athletes, coaches, and healthcare professionals.

Discussion

After looking at all of the research, one thing becomes pretty clear. Running can have both positive and negative effects on mental health. Most studies found that running helps people manage stress, improve mood, and feel better overall. This was especially true among recreational runners and people who exercised consistently without extreme training demands.

At the same time, several studies showed that the experience of competitive athletes can be very different. High training loads, pressure to perform, injuries, and constant expectations can create stress that recreational runners may never experience. While many athletes still benefit from running, some also face challenges such as anxiety, burnout, emotional exhaustion, and depression.

Another trend that appeared throughout the research was the importance of balance. Researchers repeatedly found that training itself is not the problem. In many cases, the problem develops when athletes train too much and do not allow enough time for recovery. This suggests that success in running is not simply about training harder. It is also about knowing when to rest and recover.

These findings suggest that athletes, coaches, and healthcare professionals should give mental health the same attention as physical health. While consistent running can improve well-being, maintaining a healthy balance between training and recovery appears to be equally important for long-term success and overall mental health.

One thing that seems to be missing from some areas of the research is a better understanding of how individual athletes respond to stress. Two athletes can complete the exact same training program and have very different experiences. Future studies could focus more on why some athletes thrive under pressure while others struggle with burnout or mental health concerns.

Future Research

Although researchers have made significant progress in understanding the relationship between running and mental health, several important questions remain unanswered.

Future research should continue looking at the connection between training volume and mental health. While researchers know that overtraining can be harmful, there is still no clear answer for exactly when healthy training becomes excessive training. More studies on college athletes could help answer some of these questions.

Researchers should also continue studying mental health after athletic careers end. Several studies found that former athletes can still struggle with anxiety and depression, but there is still a lot that is not fully understood. Learning more about these experiences could help schools, teams, and organizations provide better support for athletes during major life transitions.

Another area that deserves more attention is mental health education. Many athletes understand how to prevent physical injuries, but they may know very little about recognizing burnout, anxiety, or depression. Future research could explore ways to improve mental health awareness within sports programs.

Conclusion

The research reviewed in this paper shows that long-distance running can be both helpful and challenging when it comes to mental health. Many studies found that running can reduce stress, improve mood, and support overall well-being. These benefits help explain why running is often recommended as a healthy activity.

At the same time, the research also showed that competitive athletes may face unique mental health challenges. High training loads, pressure to perform, injuries, and burnout can all affect mental health. Several studies found that anxiety, depression, and emotional exhaustion are real concerns among endurance athletes.

Overall, running appears to be most beneficial when training is balanced with proper recovery and support. Understanding both the benefits and the risks can help athletes, coaches, and healthcare professionals create healthier environments where athletes can succeed both physically and mentally.
