The Relationship Between Race, Education, and Economic Status

A group literature review synthesizing nine sources on whether educational attainment and career advancement translate into stable middle-class outcomes and identity for Black Americans.

Group research Social inequality 9 sources synthesized
Contextual Statement

[Edit this paragraph with your specific course, term, and role — e.g., what section you researched, wrote, or led.] I wrote this literature review as part of a group research project studying the relationship between education, career, and identity in the Black middle class. Our group built on existing scholarship to design a survey study of our own, and this review was the foundation for that project — it's what let us ask sharper questions and make sense of what our respondents told us, instead of starting from assumptions.

Putting this review together meant reading across very different kinds of sources — a thesis on adolescent identity formation, national survey data on downward mobility, a workplace-demands study, a historical piece on the federal workforce — and finding the thread that connected them: that education and career success are supposed to be a reliable path into a stable middle-class identity, but the research shows that path is far less reliable for Black Americans than the narrative around it suggests. Organizing nine sources into a coherent argument, rather than a list of summaries, meant deciding which studies spoke to each other, where they disagreed, and what was still missing from the research.

This artifact demonstrates a different set of skills than the other two pieces in this portfolio: collaborative research design, synthesizing across disciplines (education, sociology, and economics), and building toward a research question rather than just reporting on one. It also reflects something I care about beyond athletics — understanding how systems that are supposed to reward effort fairly sometimes don't, and taking that seriously enough to study it rather than just accept the narrative at face value.

The Black middle class standards are often fueled by educational benchmarks and career experiences/opportunities. Many believe that getting a good education and finding stable, well-paying jobs can lead to financial security, social respect, and a stronger sense of identity. However, others argue that even with high levels of education and good jobs, Black Americans still face racism and barriers that affect their daily experiences and how they see themselves.

This literature review examines how education and career influence class status, identity, and social perception within the Black middle class. It explores research on the relationship between educational attainment, career advancement, and racial inequality, while also considering how these factors shape both self-identity and how others view Black professionals. Our group's study, Education, Career, and Identity in the Black Middle Class, builds on this research by collecting survey data to understand how people perceive the connection between education, opportunity, and identity in the Black community. By analyzing these perspectives, we aim to highlight whether educational and career achievements truly lead to equal experiences or if systemic barriers continue to impact life in the Black middle class.

Literature Review

In Plain Sight: How Social Images in Education Shape the Social Identity of Black Students — Kandyce Cabacar

In her thesis, In Plain Sight: How Social Images in Education Shape the Social Identity of Black Students, Cabacar (2016) examines how media representations, neighborhood structures, school practices, and peer interactions collectively shape the social identity development of Black students. Drawing on a wide body of scholarship, Cabacar (2016) argues that negative social images of Black people, which are formed through persistent media stereotypes and reinforced by segregated housing patterns, become internalized before children even enter school. Once in educational settings, these images are reproduced through academic tracking, racially divided peer groups, and teacher expectations that privilege White students. Research referenced by Cabacar (2016) suggests that Black children must navigate school environments that implicitly normalize whiteness and associate Blackness with lower academic and social value, creating ongoing identity conflict. This dynamic often requires Black students to engage in cultural straddling or code-switching to gain access to academic resources without appearing to abandon their racial identity.

Cabacar also highlights the significance of peer relationships in shaping identity. Studies she reviews indicate that students frequently choose same-race friendships for emotional safety and affirmation, yet Black students placed in predominantly White, higher-track classes may distance themselves from other Black peers in order to justify their academic placement. Findings summarized by Cabacar also show that Black students often perceive schools as socially threatening spaces due to stereotype threat and racially biased expectations. Teachers' perceptions emerge as especially influential; according to the research she synthesizes, teachers often interpret Black students as less capable or less academically engaged, echoing broader societal stereotypes. These school-based interactions feed into a cycle in which negative social images continue to shape students' developing sense of self.

Overall, Cabacar's (2016) thesis provides a compelling synthesis of research, illustrating how schools act as powerful sites of racial socialization that influence how Black youth come to understand their place in society. She argues that social images form a continuum and are cohesive and well-supported by the studies she integrates. However, the thesis is limited by its reliance on existing empirical research rather than new data. Much of the analysis depends on Cabacar's interpretation of prior studies, and the absence of direct qualitative voices restricts the depth of the conclusions.

Evidence-Supported Interventions Associated with Black Students' Educational Outcomes — Michelle R. Same, Nicole I. Guarino, Max Pardo, Deaweh Benson, Kyle Fagan & Jim Lindsay

The REL Midwest report, Evidence-supported Interventions Associated with Black Students' Educational Outcomes, conducted by researchers Same et al. (2018), reviews 22 studies on 20 K–12 strategies and helps explain why many people see schooling as a pathway into middle-class careers and identity for Black families. On academics, practices like focused instructional time, formative assessment, and certified teachers align with the belief that measurable gains open doors to selective colleges and professional roles; on climate and identity, a self-affirmation activity in higher-threat schools led Black students who wrote about personally important values to score significantly higher on a language usage test, emphasizing how belonging can protect achievement in racialized settings (Same et al., 2018). On social capital, participation in urban debate leagues is associated with scoring above English and reading ACT benchmarks and higher graduation rates, which maps debate, research, and public speaking to white-collar expectations (Same et al., 2018).

At the same time, the authors caution that none of the interventions described in the report have yet demonstrated clear, unequivocal evidence of causing beneficial results for Black students, so the narrative that educational attainment reliably translates into career opportunity and a Black middle-class identity rests here on promising associations rather than proven causal pathways, and must be understood alongside persistent access gaps and disciplinary disparities that shape how school success converts into adult outcomes.

Black-White Achievement Gap: Role of Race, School Urbanity, and Parental Education — Shervin Assari, Abbas Mardani, Maryam Maleki, Shanika Boyce & Mohsen Bazargan

In their study, Assari et al. (2021) examine how the benefits of parental education differ for Black and White students, focusing on the concept of "diminished returns." Using a nationally representative sample of 10th graders from the Education Longitudinal Study, the authors find that while higher parental education predicts better reading and math outcomes overall, these gains are smaller for Black students in urban schools. They note that at urban schools, Black students remain at educational risk despite their highly educated parents, highlighting that even well-educated Black families do not see the same academic payoff as their White peers. This finding challenges the belief that education alone secures upward mobility and a stable middle-class identity for Black families.

The study suggests that structural inequalities within urban schools, such as unequal resources, teacher bias, and tracking, limit how educational attainment translates into opportunity. A major strength of this source is its clear urban–suburban comparison, which shows that these diminished returns are not inevitable but context-dependent, tied to environments that perpetuate racial disparities. However, the study's cross-sectional nature limits causal interpretation, and it measures academic performance rather than long-term career outcomes. Overall, Assari et al. (2021) provide critical evidence that, while educational attainment remains central to middle-class aspirations, systemic barriers continue to shape how that education translates into real career and identity outcomes for Black students and families.

Educational Disparities Among Racial and Ethnic Minority Youth in the United States — Lauren Bushnell

Bushnell (2021) explores how systemic inequalities in the U.S. education system — uneven school funding, racial segregation, and differences in teacher quality — continue to disadvantage Black and Hispanic students in particular. These disparities have been shown to lead to lower test scores, graduation rates, and college enrollment, all factors associated with obtaining advanced career opportunities and upward mobility. The article also looks at practices meant to close these gaps, such as mentorship programs, early education initiatives, and efforts to diversify the teaching workforce. While these approaches show promise, Bushnell (2021) notes that major achievement gaps and unequal access to these resources are still evident in these communities.

For the Black middle class, this highlights how educational attainment can be both a sign of progress and a reminder of the barriers that must be overcome to achieve it. This article's strength is its overview of the causes and potential solutions to educational inequality, but it lacks deeper insight into how these experiences may shape Black and Hispanic students' identity beyond academic and economic outcomes. Bushnell's (2021) article shows that unequal access to quality education continues to shape the opportunities, challenges, and self-perceptions of the Black middle class.

Continuing Social Constraints in Education Agency: The School Choices and Experiences of Middle-Class African American Families in Albany, NY — Paul Knudson

Knudson (2021) examines how middle-class African American families navigate school choice within racially unequal educational systems. Drawing on nineteen interviews with parents in Albany, New York, she finds that even well-educated, professionally established Black families face barriers that limit their agency when selecting schools. Parents describe feeling excluded from predominantly White school communities and express concerns about segregation, unequal school quality, and racialized treatment that influences where they believe their children will be safe and supported (Knudson, 2021). These challenges reveal that racial inequality continues to structure educational opportunities, even for families with middle-class resources.

A key strength of Knudson's work is her focus on lived experience, which shows how Black middle-class families must engage in constant strategic decision-making to avoid discrimination and secure quality education — moving districts, enrolling in charter schools, or leveraging social networks because available options feel limited or inequitable (Knudson, 2021). This demonstrates that their agency is not absent but constrained by structural racism. However, the study's small sample and narrow geographic focus limit how widely the findings can be generalized. Still, Knudson's work shows that middle-class status does not guarantee equal educational access for African American families, who continue to face exclusion, unequal treatment, and structural constraints that shape their children's schooling opportunities.

Highly Educated Black Americans Report Higher Than Expected Perceived Job Demands — Tiffany Hogan, Amy Mancia, Kanah Ndiaye, Brenda Rodriguez, Babak Najand, Hossein Zare & Shervin Assari

Hogan et al. (2023) examine the relationship between education and workplace experiences, finding that highly educated Black workers frequently encounter greater pressure, stress, and responsibility than their White counterparts with equivalent educational attainment. The study indicates that Black professionals do not consistently receive equivalent comfort or support after securing employment, despite the expectation that education facilitates advancement into better careers. Many respondents believe they have to prove themselves more, or work harder, merely to be viewed as equal to their peers.

This finding is directly relevant to the Black middle class since it illustrates that earning a degree and a good job doesn't mean escaping extra race-related expectations. Education can help someone get a job, but it doesn't guarantee equal treatment once they're there. A strength of the study is that it looks at people's real, everyday experiences at work rather than just pay and job titles; a limitation is that it doesn't fully explain why Black workers feel heavier job demands — whether it's discrimination, underrepresentation in specific sectors, or other factors. Still, it shows that Black workers often face problems their White peers don't, even in middle-class jobs.

Black and Hispanic Americans, Those with Less Education, Are More Likely to Fall Out of the Middle Class Each Year — Rakesh Kochhar & Stella Sechopoulos

Kochhar and Sechopoulos (2022) examine how race and education influence the stability of middle-class status in the United States. The authors explain that Black and Hispanic adults are more likely than White and Asian adults to fall out of the middle class each year, and that individuals with lower levels of education face an even greater risk of downward mobility. From 2000 to 2021, about one in five Black and Hispanic adults moved down from the middle-income tier every year, compared to smaller shares of White and Asian adults; adults without a high school diploma were nearly three times as likely as college graduates to experience economic decline.

Kochhar and Sechopoulos highlight that while education can increase economic stability, racial and ethnic disparities persist even among those with similar education levels. Although the article clearly presents data on how race and education affect income mobility, it does not go into detail about the underlying causes of these disparities — systemic racism, wealth inequality, and barriers to education and employment — which leaves gaps for future research to explore.

The Federal Workforce, The Creation of the Black Middle Class, and Its Tenuous Future — Joseph Dean

Dean (2025) looks at the federal government's historical role in building the Black middle class. Starting in the mid-20th century, the federal government was one of the largest and most stable employers of Black workers, which helped many Black families enter the middle class even as private companies continued to discriminate in hiring (Dean, 2025). These jobs offered consistent pay, pensions, and health benefits that made it easier for Black families to build wealth — owning a home, or giving the next generation a chance at college. Dean (2025) describes this as a lifeline that created opportunities where few existed, but also stresses that this success is not guaranteed to last or benefit everyone.

As federal employment has shifted, it has become harder for younger Black workers to access the same opportunities earlier generations had, raising a broader concern: when stable middle-class positions disappear, racial inequality tends to increase. Dean (2025) argues that sustaining a strong Black middle class requires policy that ensures fair hiring, pay, and job protections. A strength of the piece is how clearly it traces federal employment's role in shaping the Black middle class; a weakness is that it reads more like reflective commentary than research, offering less empirical evidence to support its claims.

Shining a Light on the Black Middle Class — LesLeigh D. Ford

Ford (2023) shows that Black middle-class growth has been driven by greater access to opportunity, but remains limited by structural inequality. While the Black middle class has grown steadily over the past 50 years, Ford argues that framing hides persistent systemic racism that blocks wealth accumulation and long-term security. Middle-class status is typically defined by education, income, and homeownership, but the research shows that status doesn't carry the same stability for Black families: Black households hold significantly less wealth than White households with similar incomes (Ford, 2023).

That means even when Black families match White families' education levels and salaries, they often don't maintain the same financial footing, because they lack comparable financial safety nets — savings, home equity, or inheritance. Ford traces this gap back to long-term structural barriers: discriminatory housing policies, unequal access to credit, and exclusion from wealth-building programs like the GI Bill. A strength of Ford's article is how clearly it connects historical inequality to present-day wealth gaps; a weakness is that it relies heavily on broad claims without detailed regional or demographic data.

Examining the Loss of Wealth and Downward Mobility of African Americans — Jessica Welburn Paige

Paige (2022) examines the challenges Black families face in maintaining middle-class status compared to their White counterparts. Her research indicates that Black individuals raised in middle-class households are more likely to experience job loss or downward mobility as they enter adulthood, which she connects to the racial wealth gap, uneven housing options, and a lack of stable, well-paying jobs. Even when Black families match White families' income and education, they don't always receive the same financial support, which leaves them more vulnerable during job loss or unexpected expenses.

Paige's report is effective because it draws on multiple national studies and datasets to show that a Black middle class exists but remains significantly more vulnerable than often assumed. A limitation is that it primarily synthesizes existing studies rather than presenting new data. Still, her assessment makes clear that racial disparity continues to make career and educational advancement harder for Black Americans, even when they follow the expected "path to success."

Concluding Thoughts on Inequality and Upward Mobility

Across these studies, the same pattern appears: education and career success help shape Black middle-class identity, but they do not guarantee equal opportunity or long-term stability. Research on schooling (Cabacar, 2016; Same et al., 2018; Bushnell, 2021) shows that Black students face unequal environments and limited returns on academic achievement, even when their families have middle-class resources (Assari et al., 2021). In adulthood, this continues as Black professionals report higher pressure and unequal treatment in the workplace (Hogan et al., 2023), and middle-class parents still encounter barriers when choosing schools (Knudson, 2021). Economic studies (Kochhar & Sechopoulos, 2022; Ford, 2023; Paige, 2022) further reveal greater vulnerability to wealth loss, economic instability, and downward mobility across generations.

Taken together, the literature shows that educational and career achievements matter for mobility, but they operate within systems that continue to limit how far those achievements can carry Black families. This makes the Black middle class uniquely exposed to instability, as working hard, earning degrees, and securing professional jobs does not always translate into the same security, recognition, or long-term wealth that White families with similar credentials experience. These insights also connect directly to our group's study. By collecting survey responses on how people perceive the links between education, opportunity, and identity, we add real-life perspectives to understanding these long-standing patterns, helping show whether people still view education as the primary pathway into the middle class, or whether they recognize the ongoing structural barriers documented in the literature. Ultimately, both the research and our data point to the same conclusion: progress is real, but inequality remains deeply rooted, and understanding the Black middle class requires looking beyond individual effort to the broader systems that shape access, mobility, and identity.
